Client project · Ministry of Health of Chile · Nov 2025–now

Why weren't people using the digital health platforms? Diagnosing the system behind the screens.

As Product & Strategic Designer in the Digital Health team, I led an experience study across seven national platforms — and turned it into a DesignOps and product strategy plan.

Role

Product & Strategic Designer, Digital Health team

Scope

7 national platforms · teams, workflows and users

Methods

Interviews, heuristic evaluation, journey & service blueprint, co-design workshops

Outcome

Diagnosis report and a 12-week plan proposal — partly implemented, rest in backlog

01My role

  • Planned and led the study end to end: interviews, heuristic evaluation, journey mapping and synthesis.
  • Proposed a 12-week strategic design plan and facilitated co-design and prioritisation workshops for Telecomité, Teleinformes and Teleinterconsulta.
  • Wrote the experience analysis report and presented findings and next steps to the team.
  • Lead product designer for Teleinformes and oncology tracking: digitising legacy workflows so citizens can complete procedures online.
  • Work with Product Owners on user stories and prioritisation, and propose design governance and unified documentation across squads.

Shared with care: internal details and quotes are summarised to protect people and institutional information.

02Context

The Digital Health team runs critical platforms used by thousands of citizens and health workers. But adoption was at risk.

There were no systematic analytics, design capacity was insufficient and support was overloaded. Before designing new screens, we needed to understand why existing platforms weren't being adopted — and what in the way the team worked was causing it.

  • Why do users resist the platforms even though they're available?
  • Which workflow barriers stop teams from doing user-centred design?
  • How do structural constraints affect development and satisfaction?
  • Where are the gaps between user needs and platform capabilities?

04Findings

The biggest error wasn't a bug. It was that people simply didn't use the platforms.

Users

Dependent on constant training

Dense interfaces and technical language meant users couldn't work alone. Many kept their own spreadsheets and copied data into the platform at the end of the day — driving calls and an overloaded support team.

Workflow

Fragmented platforms, manual processes

Disconnected tools forced teams into micro-tasks with no end-to-end view. Approvals ran over email across many teams, without shared documentation or standards.

Experience

Design reduced to “making it pretty”

With no UX metrics and unclear user stories, design arrived late as a cosmetic layer and development was improvised — limiting the long-term product vision.

From diagnosis to a proposed 12-week strategic design plan: four phases to move the team from contingency mode to a shared way of working.

Systemic architectureStandard information structure and cross-platform navigation flows.
Change managementKnowledge transfer and training so the internal team can sustain it.

Note: this plan was a proposal and is being carried out partially — with limited resources, the team prioritised other work such as the Telecomité redesign, and the remaining actions are in the backlog.

08Takeaways & next steps

A diagnosis is only as useful as what the team can absorb. The biggest lesson: design maturity grows in small, visible wins — not in one big plan.

Done

  • Telecomité redesign prioritised and in progress, using the blueprint and co-design outputs.
  • Prioritisation workshops with owners and dates per platform.
In the backlog
  • Institutional design system across platforms.
  • Basic analytics and experience metrics in production.
  • Scaled DesignOps training and guidelines.
What I learned
  • Tie each recommendation to a platform the team already cares about.
  • Leave light, reusable artefacts (templates, checklists) so work continues without design capacity.
  • Measure the before: without a baseline, progress is invisible.

Client project · National Treasury of Chile (TGR) · Apr–Oct 2025

From static PDFs to transparent public finance: designing the Financial-Accounting Portal.

As Lead UX/UI Designer, I designed how the Treasury's financial and accounting information could move from scattered, manual reports to a self-managed portal that citizens, institutions and analysts can actually explore.

Role

Lead UX/UI Designer (Senior Product Designer)

Client

National Treasury of Chile · State Modernisation Agenda

Timeline

Apr – Oct 2025 · research & UX (2 months), UI, handoff & implementation (5 months)

Outcome

Portal, publication manager and design system designed and handed off · not released

01My role

  • Led end-to-end UX/UI for the public portal and its internal publication manager.
  • Planned and facilitated the Lean Inception workshops, including card sorting and co-design of categories.
  • Ran qualitative research and benchmarking, and authored the usability report.
  • Defined the information architecture, wireframes and high-fidelity UI, and built the design system.
  • Wrote QA test cases, ran UAT and prepared the launch strategy with the communications team.

02Context

Public financial data was valuable — but almost impossible to find, read or reuse.

Without a data governance framework, the Treasury published information through manual, fragmented workflows. Data arrived late and inconsistently, and citizens, journalists and researchers faced static PDFs with no analytical context. The goal: turn an internal efficiency initiative into a tool for proactive transparency and civic trust.

BeforeA growing list of static PDF reports, manual uploads and a section users struggled to find.
→
ProposedInteractive, contextualised visualisations, traceable data and downloads in CSV/Excel — managed by the Treasury itself.

03Research

Listening to both sides of the data: the people who produce it and the people who need it.

9

Internal Treasury divisions: needs, processes and structural issues of the current site.

7

Coordinators and electricity generation and distribution companies.

2

Similar public projects: Dipres Open Budget and the Ministry of Finance.

6

Benchmark references

Compared on usability, accessibility, data visualisation and transparency.

Business

The Treasury's work wasn't visible

Data uploads weren't automated and the legacy interface showed information as an ever-growing list, hiding the institution's role in public finance.

Users

Hard to find, hard to use

The main pain was locating the section at all. Once there, users wanted autonomy: processable formats like Excel plus charts and tables with context.

Benchmark

Transparency is a design feature

The best references combined clear dashboards, filters, downloads in several formats, explicit data sources and regular updates.

2.7/5

Baseline business satisfaction with the existing site. Stakeholders valued the reports — the interface was what made them hard to search and understand.

02Challenge

Patients’ health information lived in many places: exams, history and appointments, each with its own channel.

The goal was to digitalise and centralise it in a single platform, usable from any device, with key features like viewing results, managing appointments and adding family members — while keeping high standards of security and privacy.

Business

Little traceability, high-effort channels

Patients couldn’t follow their own process, and in-person channels required too much effort. People compared prices but didn’t convert.

“Who should I see?”

Patients needed guidance to choose doctors, real availability of appointments and up-to-date information before going to a clinic.

Too much information on screen; people wanted better grouping, downloadable records and integrated care for the whole family.

Partner project · Futurium × SRH Berlin · 2025–2026

The Futures Kaleidoscope: a game that makes AI futures tangible.

In the M.A. Strategic Design at SRH Berlin, our team researched how people relate to AI and the future, co-created a game for Futurium and ran it as a three-hour workshop at the museum’s sold-out Open Lab Night — later adapted for the IHK Tech Summit.

Role

Strategic designer, game designer & facilitator

Context

Academic project with Futurium · team of three (Sofía Guevara, Anshu Kumari, Felipe Medina)

Timeline

Oct 2025 – now

Shown at

Futurium Open Lab Night “AI in Love and Life” · IHK Tech Summit

01My role

  • Conducted interviews on people’s perceptions of AI, trust and the future as part of the research team.
  • Did speculative research on AI ethics and emerging technologies to frame the scenarios.
  • Team role: “The Strategist & Navigator” — bridging complex problem-solving with clear, functional experiences aligned with partner goals.
  • Co-designed the game mechanics, materials and formats for different visit lengths.
  • Facilitated alignment workshops with institutional stakeholders and shaped a strategic roadmap from iterative testing loops.

02Challenge

How can we make our future with AI more tangible?

Futurium positions itself as a neutral space for exploration, not answers. The challenge was to invite visitors of all ages to form their own opinion about AI — and to make that reflection active, social and fun.

03Research

We reached 150+ people across five decades of ages — layering methods to understand both the museum and how people feel about AI.

Desk researchImmersion & observationIn-depth interviewsCultural probesSurveysExpert & staff conversations
Museum

Interaction draws people in

Interactive exhibits gathered small groups of 2–4, static ones 1–2. Children looked for anything they could touch; average time per exhibit was 5–15 minutes.

Visitors

Opinions, but no space to reflect

People wanted to propose solutions and critique the artefacts, but there was little room for active reflection. Few were familiar with museums about the future.

AI perceptions

Familiarity lowers fear

Adults were more sceptical than younger visitors. Interviewees saw AI as a tool, questioned its trustworthiness and privacy, and worried about creative work.

✦

Design principle: storytelling at the core, within Futurium’s ethos of open exploration — a game, not a lecture.

04The game

Berlin, 2040. AI wants into your courtroom, clinic and classroom. You decide how far.

  1. Three sectorsScenario cards for Law & Justice, Health & Well-being and Education, each with a concrete dilemma.
  2. One vote, three futuresPlayers choose Full AI, Limited AI or No AI — each with its own implications.
  3. Secret missionsHidden goals push players to negotiate; completing one earns a joker chip for the next round.
  4. A live, shared BerlinA shared screen shows the live tally of every vote, turning individual choices into a collective future.

Cerro Poqui: infrastructure and signage to protect a nature sanctuary.

A team project to protect the biodiversity of a protected area in Chile and educate its visitors — through a walkway, a viewpoint and an illustrated interpretive panel.

01My role

  • Industrial and graphic designer in a four-person team, from site analysis to final proposal.
  • Illustration of native flora and fauna and design of the interpretive panel.
  • Design of the infrastructure — walkway, handrails and viewpoint — and material quantification.

02Context

A unique ecosystem under pressure from its own popularity.

The Cerro Poqui Nature Sanctuary is home to a rich ecosystem of flora and fauna. The Chalé de Piedra sits in a privileged spot between the hygrophilous forest and Los Calabozos creek, habitat of species like the Cantillana spiny-chested frog. Growing visitor numbers were endangering the native species — so the place needed to be revalued, its territory limited and conservation encouraged responsibly.

03Process

From the territory to the detail: understanding the site, telling its story and building lightly on it.

  1. Site analysisMapping, elevation profiles, sketches and an actor map of the sanctuary and its visitors.
  2. IllustrationFlora and fauna illustrated step by step, from line drawing to final colour.
  3. Interpretive panel“El bosque del río”: a panel that explains the wetland ecosystem and its species.
  4. InfrastructureWalkway, handrail and viewpoint construction systems, with quantification of materials.

04Outcome

Infrastructure that protects while it teaches — now being built in the sanctuary.

1st

First place at the Chile Diseño Awards 2024 in spatial design, and finalist at LADAWARDS 2022 in the signage category. The project is now being built: the signage is already installed and the rest of the infrastructure is under construction.

The Futures Kaleidoscope

A board game where visitors collectively shape Berlin in 2040 through real-time decisions about AI — turning passive observers into active contributors to their future.

  • 150+people reached in research across five decades of ages
  • 4rounds of testing, from paper to a live event
  • 1sold-out, three-hour workshop at Futurium’s Open Lab Night “AI in Love and Life”
Bring it to your team or event →Read the case

Played live

A sold-out Open Lab Night at Futurium

We ran the Futures Kaleidoscope as a three-hour workshop at Futurium’s Open Lab Night “AI in Love and Life”. It went really well: lively debates, a full room and people asking to keep playing.

Why a game?

44.7%

trust their ability to spot AI bias…

32.9%

…but only this many understand how AI works

40%

consistently think about the privacy of their data when using AI

Our survey and interviews revealed a gap between confidence in using AI and understanding it. We needed something that made abstract systems tangible — and debate feel like play, not like a survey.

What’s in the box

Scenario cards

Four facets of Berlin 2040 — law & justice, health, education and relationships — each with a concrete AI dilemma.

Voting chips

Total ban, acceleration or joker: each chip can be used only once, so every vote is a strategic choice.

Secret missions

Hidden agendas that spark debate and negotiation; completing one earns a joker chip.

Reflection cards

Positive and negative outcomes of each decision, told through recurring characters.

Wild cards

Plot twists that change the rules mid-game and keep everyone alert.

Fail early, fail cheap: four rounds of testing

28.05

Test 1 · Low fidelity

5 participants, paper tokens. Voting by percentage killed the debate — people calculated instead of arguing. We switched to clear sides, majority vote and a warm-up that became the secret missions.

03.06

Test 2 · AI playtest

A “Futurium Playtester” skill built in Claude simulated archetypes and personas to A/B test chip-based voting in an afternoon.

→

Test 3 · Expert review

A hand-built, more polished prototype tested with a game design expert in the room.

Live

Test 4 · Futurium

The first version built for an environment we didn’t control: a live event, a mixed-age audience and a room we didn’t design.

What held true across every round

  • The simpler, the better. Every version that tested well was simpler than the one before it.
  • Gamification is key. People asked to keep playing after the test had ended.
  • The warm-up is an underused design tool — it became one of the most effective moments.
  • Human creativity, not synthetic: participants left with handmade postcards as a keepsake.

Scalability

Built with Futurium. Ready for any industry.

The scenarios were created directly for and with the Futurium team: cross-cutting themes for a general public audience visiting the museum.

The game mechanics — warm-up, scenario, debate, vote and build — stay the same. What changes is the content: scenario cards and dilemmas can be rewritten for a company’s own context, to explore the futures of its industry with its teams, clients or community.
